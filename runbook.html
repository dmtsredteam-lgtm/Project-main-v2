What was built

The three projects existed and worked. None of them knew the others were there. One new service — the Arena Hub, about 600 lines of dependency-free Node — now sits between them.

It does four things: take in telemetry from every game, normalise it into the shape the SOC wall's alert pipeline already speaks, keep one leaderboard across all four games, and fan events out over Server-Sent Events — including back down to a red team laptop, so the SOC can interrupt a running game.

The governing rule

The hub is never load-bearing. Unplug it mid-show and three games keep running exactly as before — only the shared board and the cross-screen theatre stop. Every integration point was built that way deliberately, and it was tested with the hub killed and with the hub pointed at an unroutable address. Worst-case request time went from 23 ms to 26 ms.

The show, in one paragraph

A visitor sits at Laptop 02 and starts breaching Aegis Vault Systems. On the big screen an arc lights up on the globe and the AEGIS AI panel says what it just saw. The visitor fumbles a password. The wall's operation card switches to their name and a heat bar starts filling — the crowd can see how close they are to being caught before they can. They fumble another. The wall flashes SOC RESPONSE — ADAPTIVE THROTTLE ENGAGED, and 1.9 seconds later the laptop screen is taken over: session held, twelve-second countdown, clock still running. People look at the big screen, then at the laptop, and understand what a SOC does without anyone explaining it.

Try it on one machine first

Before any of this goes near the show floor, run-local.sh brings the whole booth up on a single laptop — no Docker, no npm build.

soc-wall-main/dist/ ships pre-built, so the rig needs only Node 18+ and Python 3. Two processes come up: the Arena Hub on :7788, which also serves the wall and the arcade, and the Red Team Challenge on :8000. 

cd ~/Downloads/Project
chmod +x run-local.sh simulate.sh
./run-local.sh

The script checks its prerequisites, creates a .venv and installs Flask the first time, waits for both services to actually answer rather than sleeping and hoping, prints the URLs, and tears everything down on Ctrl-C. If a service dies it says so and shows the last dozen log lines.

Both services bind 0.0.0.0 and the script prints the machine's LAN address, so the iPad, the laptops and the big screen can be four separate devices — or any phone on the same Wi-Fi can open the wall and watch. The address baked into the red team pages is the LAN one, not loopback: hand a laptop 127.0.0.1 and its browser points at itself, the SOC stream never connects, and nothing that laptop does reaches the wall. Override the detected address with LAN_IP=10.0.0.5 ./run-local.sh on a multi-homed box, and open the ports if a device cannot connect: sudo ufw allow 7788/tcp && sudo ufw allow 8000/tcp.

The simulator

simulate.sh plays a whole run against the real Flask routes — same session, same heat engine a visitor hits — so you can watch the wall react without clicking through five stages. It prints heat and posture after every action, so the terminal and the wall tell the same story side by side. 

./simulate.sh            # noisy operator: throttled repeatedly, still finishes
./simulate.sh --clean    # careful operator at human pace: SOC never interrupts
./simulate.sh --bust     # four bad passwords, contained
./simulate.sh --arcade   # drop a morning's arcade scores on the board

Put the wall on one half of the screen and the terminal on the other. The --clean run is the one to show a colleague: a tidy operator gets noticed but never held, which is what makes the throttle feel earned when it does land.

On Kali

The hub needs Node 18, which stock Kali has. Rebuilding the wall needs Node 22.13+ for Vite 8, which stock Kali usually does not — that is exactly why dist/ ships pre-built. You only need the newer Node if you change something under soc-wall-main/, and nvm install 22 is the easiest way to get it. Docker is not needed at all: the rig runs Flask directly in a virtualenv.

Architecture

Solid lines carry telemetry up. Dashed lines are the live streams back down — including the containment channel that lets the SOC act on a laptop mid-game.

Why a separate service

The arcade's board lives on Vercel KV, the challenge's in SQLite, the wall queries Wazuh. Making any one of them the hub would have tied the booth to that deploy target and to internet access. A standalone process with no dependencies runs from a USB stick on any laptop with Node 18, offline — and each game keeps its own storage as a fallback.

The alert contract

The hub emits alerts already shaped to what the wall's fetchAlerts() returns. That is the whole trick: a failed password on Laptop 02 enters alertStore.add() and the globe, the origin list and the threat timeline pick it up with zero downstream changes.

Where the attacks appear on the globe

The stations sit forty metres from the wall, so a truthful arc would be a dot on Dubai. Instead each kill-chain phase is plotted at the exit infrastructure a real operator would route it through — which is also exactly what a SOC sees in source-IP geolocation. Long arcs converging on the Dubai perimeter, and defensible if a visitor asks how it works.

Classification is DMATICS' own — plain kill-chain phases rather than any third-party framework's identifiers, so nothing on the booth screen carries another vendor's naming, and CREDENTIAL ACCESS reads across a hall in a way T1110 never did.

The SOC defence loop

This is the mechanic the brief asked for: if a password error or certain things get triggered, the SOC defence acts and interrupts the game.

Every noisy action raises a heat score on the player's session. Heat decays at 0.9 points per second, so a careful player who takes a minute per stage sheds more than the stage costs and never trips anything. A player who fumbles passwords or hammers the shell climbs.

The original hard rules are untouched — four bad portal logins, three bad SSH logins, or any sudo escalation still end a run instantly. The heat ladder adds the middle tier: the part a visitor can feel building, and the part a crowd can watch building.

Why the throttle is the good one

A bust is binary and it ends the visit. A throttle costs twelve seconds of a ten-minute clock, teaches that detection has consequences short of game-over, and — critically — happens while people are watching. It is also the only response that can fire more than once in a run.

Two details make it work rather than annoy:

  • A throttled attempt does not burn an attempt. The SOC took the time instead. A player never loses a life to the mechanic.
  • Heat drops 26 points when a throttle fires. Without that bleed-off the next action re-trips it immediately and the run becomes a slideshow.
The 1.9-second beat

The laptop shows “SOC IS RESPONDING” for 1.9 seconds before the lockout lands. This is the single most important number in the build. Without it the wall and the laptop fire together and the causal link between them is invisible. With it, the crowd sees the detection appear on the big screen, turns to look at the laptop, and then the takeover happens. Cause, then effect.

The same principle governs event ordering server-side: a detection always reaches the wall before the escalation it triggered. Getting that backwards was a real bug found during testing, and it destroyed the effect completely.

It is enforced, not just drawn

The overlay is theatre. The throttled_until timestamp in the Flask session is what means it: while a hold is active, /portal/login, /console/auth and /console/exec refuse outright. Closing the overlay or reloading gains nothing — which matters, because the crowd just watched the SOC act.

It works with the hub off

Heat is computed in the game, in Flask, and the interruption is delivered in the HTTP reply to the action that caused it. The hub only mirrors it to the wall. If the hub is down the defence still works — only the big screen goes quiet.

Running it

Order does not actually matter — everything reconnects — but this is the least confusing sequence.

  1. Arena Hub — the machine driving the big screen

    cd gisec-hub
node server.js                 # :7788

    To have the hub also serve the wall and the arcade, so the booth runs one process:

    cd soc-wall-main && npm install && npm run build
cd ../gisec-hub && node server.js
#   wall    → http://<hub>:7788/
#   arcade  → http://<hub>:7788/arcade

  2. SOC Wall — big screen, Chrome fullscreen, F11

    Edit dist/soc-config.js. It is deliberately unbundled so it can be changed on the show floor with a text editor and a refresh — no rebuild, no toolchain. 

    hub: { url: "", sameOrigin: true, port: 7788 }

    Set sameOrigin: true when the hub serves the wall; otherwise set url to the hub's LAN address. Adding ?hub=http://10.0.0.5:7788 to the wall URL overrides both — the fastest way to repoint the screen mid-show.

    Launch it with this, not by opening a tab — the flags matter:

    chromium --kiosk --autoplay-policy=no-user-gesture-required \
         http://<hub>:7788/

    --kiosk removes the tab strip and address bar, which on a 1080p panel were costing the wall about ninety pixels of height. --autoplay-policy=no-user-gesture-required is what makes the alert audio work from boot: a browser will not make a sound until the page has been interacted with, and nobody interacts with a wall display. Without the flag the wall shows a prompt in the corner and one click on the screen arms it for good.

  3. Red Team Challenge — both laptops

    cd DMATICS-Red-Team-Challenge-main
docker compose up -d --build

    HUB_URL in docker-compose.yml must be the hub's LAN address, not localhost — this runs inside a container, where localhost is the container.

    Then open each laptop once at its station URL, which is remembered in the session:

    Laptop 1 → http://<host>:8000/?station=LAPTOP-01
Laptop 2 → http://<host>:8000/?station=LAPTOP-02

    One container can serve both laptops. Two containers also works — set STATION_ID per container.

  4. Arcade — iPad

    http://<hub>:7788/arcade?hub=http://<hub>:7788

    The hub parameter is remembered in the tablet's local storage, so this is a once-per-device setup, not a daily one. Add to Home Screen for fullscreen. ?hub=off takes a device off the wall. Hosted on Vercel instead? Set NEXT_PUBLIC_GISEC_HUB and the address is baked into the deploy.

Booth settings

Three dials live in soc-wall-main/dist/soc-config.js, unbundled so they can be changed on the show floor with a text editor and a refresh.

  • timezone — every timestamp on the wall reads this zone, so the screen agrees with the clock in the hall. Set to Asia/Dubai.
  • globeMotion — true by default. A booth machine with the desktop's reduce-motion setting turned on would otherwise show a dead globe and nobody would know why. "auto" defers to the operating system.
  • hub — where the arena hub lives, if it is not serving the wall.
  • overscan — 1 by default, meaning fill the screen edge to edge. Drop it to 0.95 if the screen at the stand crops the picture.
  • sound — volume 0.7 by default. A loud hall wants 0.9, a quiet one 0.45. ?volume=0.4 or ?sound=off on the wall URL overrides it for one screen.

The arcade has the same motion override, because an iPad often ships with Reduce Motion on and that blanks its animated background: open it once with ?motion=on (the default) or ?motion=auto to follow the device. The choice is remembered on the tablet.

Any screen, any resolution

The wall is composed once at 1920×1080 and then scaled as one piece to whatever it is plugged into. Plug it in and it fits — there is nothing to configure, and no resolution it has to be told about.

This matters because the alternative does not work. A layout that re-flows to the viewport moves the boxes but not the words inside them, so on a screen it was not tuned for the text walks out of its panel. Scaling the finished surface removes the whole class of problem: there is no re-flow to get wrong, so every screen shows the same wall at a different size.

Verified identical — every panel, every gap, every glyph, measured and compared — at 1024×768, 1280×1024, 1366×768, 1600×900, 1920×1080, 1920×1200, 2560×1080, 2560×1440, 3840×2160 and in portrait.

On a screen that is not 16:9 the wall keeps its shape and the ambient background fills the band above and below it, so it reads as part of the design rather than as a black bar. Two things to know on the day:

  • Press F11. Chrome's tab strip and address bar eat ~90 px of height, and the wall scales down to fit whatever is left — so it is the browser chrome, not the layout, that costs you the most picture.
  • If the screen crops the edges, that is the television's own overscan setting. Turn it off in the TV menu if you can find it (it is usually called "Just Scan", "Screen Fit", "1:1" or "Full Pixel"). If you cannot, add ?fit=0.95 to the wall URL and the whole surface steps in from the bezel. No rebuild, no file to edit.

The two panels that used to stand still

The ACTIVE CAMPAIGN card and the DMATICS AI assessment were furniture — a hard-coded "DESERT VIPER · 14 signals · 3 targets · risk 92" and three canned sentences on a timer. Both now read from the same alert stream the globe and the timeline draw from, so the campaign name, class, affected asset, signal count, containment ratio, origin country, confidence ring and the analyst's sentence all move with the traffic.

The interesting engineering is in making it move slowly enough. The alert window holds eight items, so "which class is dominant" flips on a difference of one — wired naively the card renamed itself three times a minute, which looks more broken than the frozen version did. The campaign keeps its own ten-minute history and a challenger has to lead by four signals, or lead at all for two minutes, before the card renames. The name holds for minutes; the numbers under it change every few seconds. A booth laptop always outranks the ambient world and takes the card instantly.

What the wall sounds like

Six cues, all synthesised in the browser. There is not a single audio file in the project: nothing to lose off a USB stick, nothing that fails when the stand has no internet, nothing to decode before the first alert can be heard.

CueFires onSound
ATTENTIONseverity 8–11 — recon, a shell command, a loginrising perfect fourth, 659 → 880 Hz
ELEVATEDseverity 12–13 — password spraying, credential accessthree notes that do not resolve, 740 → 988 → 831 Hz
CRITICALseverity 14+ — privilege escalation, exfiltrationthree tritone double-stops, 932 + 1318 Hz

ALL CLEARstation releasedfalling triad, 880 → 659 → 523 Hz
SCOREan arcade score landing on the boardone sine tick at 1319 Hz, nearly subliminal

Two rules stop it becoming noise, which is what gets booth audio switched off by lunchtime on day one. The ambient telemetry is silent — the wall invents background traffic constantly to keep the globe alive between visitors and none of it is audible, so every sound the stand makes was caused by someone standing in it. And a burst is one sound: ordinary cues are held to one every 700 ms and ten per thirty seconds, so eight detections arriving together produce one chime rather than eight. Critical cues interrupt that, with their own five-second floor.

There are twenty-three decibels between the quietest cue and the loudest. A score landing is barely there; a containment is the loudest thing the stand does. Every cue is playable at the sound check page, which runs the wall's own audio code — including a button that plays the whole detection-to-containment sequence at booth timing.

The arena board

Five boards rotating every nine seconds with a progress sweep: OVERALL · PHISH · ALERTS · BREACH · RED OPS. A wall has nobody standing at it to click tabs, and the rotation is what makes a visitor stop and wait for their own game to come round. Booth staff can still tap a tab to hold one for a full cycle. A score that lands jumps to that board and pulses the row once, so a player watching from the stand sees their own name arrive.

The overall board is not a points total

Red Team maxes at 100; Breach Point can pass 2,000. Summing them would make the arcade the only game that mattered. Each game is normalised to 1,000 points at that game's current top score — so being best-in-class counts the same everywhere, and playing more games beats grinding one. That is the right incentive for a stand: it moves people between the iPad and the laptops.

Tuning on the day

All in docker-compose.yml, no rebuild:

  • Queue is long, move people through — HEAT_DECAY=1.4, THROTTLE_SECONDS=8
  • Quiet spell, want the drama — HEAT_THROTTLE=45
  • A VIP demo you do not want interrupted — HEAT_THROTTLE=200

The wall's ambient telemetry is demo: true in soc-config.js. Leave it on — booth activity arrives through the hub either way, and the ambient traffic is what keeps the globe alive between visitors.

What was verified, not just changed

  • Ten resolutions, 1024×768 to 4K and both portrait sizes — correct scale factor, surface inside the viewport, no page overflow, no clipped text.
  • All four rehearsal presets against a live hub and challenge: clean finishes, noisy finishes after cooling off, bust reaches containment, arcade fills the boards.
  • The full show with a browser watching the wall: GUARDED → ELEVATED → SEVERE, the response band walking advisory → throttle → contained, the station going red, and the run landing on the RED OPS board.
  • The arcade in a browser — a complete 60-second round under the new CSP with no console errors, and seventeen hub-address cases including the exfiltration vector and the Vercel fallback.
  • Two gunicorn workers sharing a session across twelve requests with a key set, and exactly one worker without.
  • The wall with its font host blocked, which is the GISEC-with-no-internet case. It renders in the local stacks and looks deliberate.
